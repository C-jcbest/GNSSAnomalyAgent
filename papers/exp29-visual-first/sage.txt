Abstract
Time-series anomaly detection often returns scores or intervals, while analysts need to understand the abnormal behavior and the evidence supporting it. We introduce SAGE (Specialized Analyzer Group for Expert-like Detection), a multi-agent framework for evidence-grounded diagnosis of univariate time series. Four specialized Analyzers examine point, structural, seasonal, and pattern anomalies using numerical tools and diagnostic visualizations. A Detector integrates their evidence into intervals, candidate types, and evidence-strength confidence scores; a Supervisor translates these records into analyst-facing reports. Synthetic in-context references are constructed from normal-reference training segments, reducing dependence on real anomalous demonstrations. Across Yahoo S5, KPI, and WSD, SAGE achieves an average Point-F1 of 66.26, the highest among the evaluated methods. Controlled synthetic evaluation examines localization and type diagnosis, while component ablations support the detection contribution of specialized evidence generation. In a method-blind human study, evaluators rate SAGE’s diagnostic outputs as more useful than those of the compared methods.
1
Introduction
Time-series anomaly detection supports monitoring of web services, infrastructure, and industrial processes
[
Laptev et al., 2015
,
Ren et al., 2019
]
. A detected interval is often only the start of an analyst’s investigation. The analyst must distinguish an isolated spike from a persistent level shift, determine whether periodic structure has changed, and examine why a system regards the interval as abnormal. These questions require different evidence: local statistics for contextual outliers, segment comparisons for regime changes, and frequency or shape analysis for disrupted patterns. A single anomaly score does not directly communicate this diagnostic structure.
We therefore seek to organize heterogeneous numerical and visual evidence into an explicit diagnostic record. Here,
diagnosis
means identifying abnormal temporal behavior, its location, plausible anomaly types, and supporting evidence. It does not mean establishing a causal root cause. A univariate signal may reveal a level shift without identifying the external event that caused it.
Large language and vision-language models offer a natural interface for evidence comparison and explanation. Recent systems use textual prompting, retrieved examples, or visual time-series representations for anomaly detection
[
Alnegheimish et al., 2024
,
Liu et al., 2025
,
Zhuang et al., 2024
]
. However, direct reasoning over long numerical inputs remains an awkward fit for precise statistical operations and heterogeneous anomaly families. Tool use can provide the missing numerical precision, but a diagnostic system must also decide which evidence to generate, how to compare conflicting findings, and how to preserve that evidence in its final output.
We propose SAGE, a multi-agent framework that decomposes diagnosis by anomaly family. Four Analyzers specialize in point, structural, seasonal, and pattern anomalies. Each uses dedicated numerical tools and visual representations to produce candidate regions and diagnostic evidence. A Detector consolidates these findings into anomaly intervals, candidate types, and confidence scores interpreted as evidence strength. A Supervisor turns the structured records into reports describing the signal, alarm rationale, and suggested follow-up checks. The role of the LLM is to organize and interpret tool-supported evidence, while numerical tools retain access to the original signal.
Real anomaly examples and anomaly-type annotations are often scarce, especially early in deployment. The Detector nevertheless benefits from examples that contrast expected behavior with a specific deviation when interpreting heterogeneous Analyzer evidence. SAGE therefore constructs synthetic in-context learning (ICL) references from normal-reference training segments. Rule-based perturbations create matched normal–anomalous pairs, and retrieval supplies contrasts relevant to the Analyzer-proposed types. Because each pair shares the same underlying normal pattern, the contrast highlights the injected change rather than unrelated differences between series. This provides type-specific reference evidence without relying on real anomalous demonstrations.
Our contributions are as follows:
1.
We formulate univariate anomaly diagnosis as structured evidence generation and aggregation, linking family-specific analysis to explicit records of anomaly intervals, candidate types, and supporting evidence.
2.
We develop a multi-agent workflow that combines specialized Analyzers, numerical and visual tools, compact prompt representations, and synthetic contrastive references constructed from normal training segments.
3.
We evaluate detection, type diagnosis, component contributions, backbone dependence, and human-rated report quality, showing the value of evidence-grounded organization across complementary assessments.
2
Related Work
Time-series anomaly detection.
Classical methods identify distributional or neighborhood deviations, as in Isolation Forest and LOF
[
Liu et al., 2008
,
Breunig et al., 2000
]
. Neural methods use reconstruction, latent dynamics, or attention-based discrepancies, including LSTM-AE, USAD, OmniAnomaly, and Anomaly Transformer
[
Malhotra et al., 2016
,
Audibert et al., 2020
,
Su et al., 2019
,
Xu et al., 2022
]
. TFAD further uses decomposition and time–frequency analysis to model temporal structure
[
Zhang et al., 2022a
]
. These methods provide strong detection mechanisms, but a score alone does not specify whether a deviation is point-like, structural, seasonal, or pattern-based, or record the evidence for that assignment. SAGE uses established statistical and signal-processing tools for this diagnostic purpose rather than proposing a new low-level detector.
LLM/VLM-based anomaly analysis.
PromptCast, LLMTime, and Time-LLM show how language models can be adapted to time-series forecasting
[
Xue and Salim, 2024
,
Gruver et al., 2023
,
Jin et al., 2024
]
; forecasting does not directly provide anomaly intervals or type-specific diagnostic evidence.
SigLLM evaluates language-model prompting and forecasting pipelines for time-series anomalies
[
Alnegheimish et al., 2024
]
. LLMAD combines anomaly-oriented reasoning with retrieved real examples
[
Liu et al., 2025
]
. TAMA uses visual representations and few-shot multimodal reasoning
[
Zhuang et al., 2024
]
, while TSAD-Agents explores multimodal agentic analysis
[
Xu et al., 2026
]
. SAGE combines family-specific evidence generation with explicit Detector-level aggregation. Its synthetic references pair normal prototypes with controlled perturbations, and its output separates evidence-strength scoring, type diagnosis, and report generation.
Tool-supported and multi-agent reasoning.
ReAct and Toolformer demonstrate how external tools can support language-model reasoning
[
Yao et al., 2023
,
Schick et al., 2023
]
; MetaGPT and CAMEL study collaboration through specialized roles
[
Hong et al., 2024
,
Li et al., 2023
]
. SAGE applies these principles to a domain-specific decomposition whose roles follow the evidence required by anomaly families rather than generic conversational tasks. The Analyzers express candidate locations, types, and tool outputs in a shared representation; the Detector weighs supporting and conflicting evidence with an explicit rubric rather than treating agent findings as independent votes. The contribution is this diagnostic orchestration of existing tools and model capabilities, not a new numerical detector or foundation-model architecture.

Agreement can strengthen a candidate, but confidence is not simply a count of agreeing agents, nor is it a calibrated probability.
When Analyzers disagree, the Detector weighs the underlying tool and visual evidence rather than voting over agent outputs. A weak, isolated deviation can receive less support than a candidate with a clear magnitude or persistent change corroborated by another source. Retaining the interval and candidate types makes this judgment inspectable.
The Detector uses tool-reported indices to preserve localization precision and visual evidence to assess the extent of a candidate. Its prompt also permits reporting a missed candidate when the provided data or chart supports it. Thus, aggregation is evidence-guided but is not a formal constraint guaranteeing that every output is a member of the Analyzer candidate set. The prompts encode the intended behavior; they do not establish factual correctness of the generated interpretation.

3.4
Synthetic in-context references from normal data
Figure 2:
Offline construction and online retrieval of synthetic contrastive references. DTW retrieves normal prototypes; Analyzer-proposed types determine which associated perturbations are passed to the Detector.
Real anomaly examples can be scarce or poorly annotated. SAGE builds references from the training portion of each time series, excluding segments that overlap labeled anomalies. It selects representative normal subsequences with
k
k
-medoids, retaining observed prototypes rather than averaged centroids. Each prototype receives one rule-based perturbation for each of the nine anomaly types. These injection rules construct references; they are not applied as inference-time detection rules. The original and perturbed signals form a contrastive pair with an injected type label and a summary of changed tool evidence (Figure
2
).
At inference time, Dynamic Time Warping with LB_Keogh pruning retrieves the three closest normal prototypes
[
Sakoe and Chiba, 1978
,
Keogh, 2002
]
. Analyzer-proposed candidate types select the associated synthetic contrasts. These references are supplied only to the Detector to support evidence comparison, confidence scoring, and type ranking. Neither retrieval nor type selection uses test labels or true test anomaly types. During reference construction, binary training labels are used only to exclude anomalous segments; real anomalous segments are not supplied as demonstrations.
The synthetic type evaluation is separate: it disables synthetic ICL for SAGE, so its test cases receive no retrieved synthetic demonstrations. This evaluates the existing tools and aggregation process on controlled perturbations, rather than reference-label copying. The taxonomy and perturbation design nevertheless define the scope of the evaluation; success does not establish coverage of all real failure modes.
4
Experimental Evaluation
4.1
Setup and evaluation protocol
We evaluate Yahoo S5 (367 series, average length approximately 1,400), KPI (29 series, approximately 100,000), and WSD (111 series, approximately 17,000)
[
Yahoo Labs, 2015
,
Li et al., 2022
,
Zhang et al., 2022b
]
. Yahoo contains real web traffic (A1) and synthetic subsets (A2–A4); KPI and WSD contain operational infrastructure and web-service metrics. All three provide binary anomaly labels. Training and test data preserve temporal order. Yahoo uses 40% of each series for training and 60% for testing; KPI and WSD use 70% for training and 5% for testing, excluding the remaining 25% to limit API inference time and cost. Their reported results therefore concern these reduced test portions. SAGE uses the training portion for normal-reference construction, not parameter learning.
The LangGraph workflow uses non-overlapping 400-point windows, temperature 0.0, and retrieval
k
=
3
k=3
. The common backbone for SAGE and the LLM/VLM comparisons is Claude Sonnet 4.6. Baseline input representations, prompting logic, ICL strategies, and post-processing are retained as closely as possible when replacing the backbone. This controls model identity, but does not equalize inference budgets. We compare eight ML/DL methods and four LLM/VLM systems. Additional settings and backbone experiments are given in Appendices
B
and
E
.
We report Point-F1, point-adjusted F1 (PA-F1), Affiliation-F1
[
Huet et al., 2022
]
, and Delayed-F1 with delay parameter
k
=
3
k=3
, all as percentages. Point-F1 computes precision and recall at the time-point level and penalizes imprecise boundaries and false positives. PA-F1 gives segment-level credit when a predicted anomaly overlaps a labeled anomaly, providing a more relaxed view of detection. Affiliation-F1 measures the temporal proximity of predicted and labeled anomaly intervals. Delayed-F1 assesses timely detection by treating an anomaly as detected when its first prediction occurs within three time steps after onset. Together, these metrics distinguish point-level localization, segment coverage, temporal proximity, and alert delay.

Binary benchmark labels cannot establish type-diagnosis quality. We therefore evaluate 156 synthetic samples spanning the nine injected types, with synthetic ICL disabled for SAGE. Detection recall is the fraction for which the Analyzer assigned to the injected family returns an evidence interval overlapping the labeled anomaly. The reported type-agreement metric compares the highest-confidence predicted type with the injected type on detected cases. Its within-family and overall aggregation are not specified, so we treat the reported rates as descriptive summaries (Appendix
A

Table

Pattern anomalies are frequently located (97% evidence recall), but have the lowest reported type agreement (31%). Seasonal evidence recall is 54%. These differences show that finding an abnormal region and assigning its injected type are distinct tasks, especially for pattern changes.
On the same synthetic set, SAGE has the strongest Point-F1 and PA-F1, whereas TAMA leads on Delayed-F1 and Affiliation-F1. TAMA’s higher Delayed-F1 and Affiliation-F1 indicate that its predictions often occur soon after anomaly onset and near the labeled intervals, respectively. This is consistent with visual representations capturing approximate anomaly extent, even though its lower Point-F1 and PA-F1 indicate less precise localization. The aggregate comparison does not isolate visual input as the cause. An auxiliary, single-author annotation covers 300 benchmark test intervals, of which 251 are point anomalies. Its aggregate and per-type agreement summaries do not reconcile as a pooled proportion; we retain them only as supplementary records (Appendix

5
Analysis
5.1
Contributions of tools, specialization, vision, and references
Figure 3:
Point-F1 component ablations on Yahoo S5, KPI, and WSD. Bars show the full system and the removal of tools, Analyzer specialization (Multi), visual evidence, or synthetic in-context references. Detailed results for all metrics are in Appendix
E
.
Figure
3
shows the Point-F1 component ablations. On Yahoo, removing numerical tools reduces Point-F1 from 79.69 to 34.79, and removing Analyzer specialization reduces it to 41.08. These drops support the value of numerical evidence and family-specific analysis for heterogeneous anomalies. On KPI, removing visual evidence lowers Point-F1 from 60.97 to 47.83, and removing ICL lowers it to 49.21. The corresponding WSD scores fall from 58.12 to 52.97 and 54.87.
Specialization has a smaller Point-F1 effect on KPI and WSD. Some segment metrics even improve when it is removed: for example, WSD PA-F1 rises from 95.68 to 98.87. These observations qualify the contribution. Specialized analysis improves point-level localization in these experiments, but does not uniformly improve every overlap-based metric. The ablations also alter the workflow and its computational budget; they are not a matched-cost test of agent count alone. Removing tools establishes their importance, but does not isolate the benefit of LLM aggregation over a non-LLM aggregator receiving the same evidence.
Without ICL, SAGE still exceeds LLMAD with ICL on KPI (49.21 versus 19.47 Point-F1) and WSD (54.87 versus 41.59). On Yahoo, however, zero-shot SAGE scores 71.21 versus 76.28 for zero-shot LLMAD. Retrieved references and architecture therefore contribute differently across datasets. Removing ICL tests dependence on retrieved references; it does not test unseen anomaly types or robustness to misleading references. Full ablation and zero-shot tables appear in Appendix

6
Discussion and Conclusion
SAGE organizes univariate anomaly analysis around specialized evidence generation, Detector-level integration, and structured reporting. The experiments show gains in average detection performance and higher human ratings of diagnostic outputs. Controlled type evaluation reveals that finding an abnormal region and assigning its type remain distinct challenges. The framework is best understood as evidence-grounded support for analysts, with confidence representing evidence strength and recommendations identifying follow-up checks.
Our conclusions are empirical and restricted to the evaluated univariate domains and anomaly settings. The framework remains limited by inference cost, backbone dependence, and a fixed anomaly taxonomy. Extending diagnosis to multivariate data will require evidence about variable contributions and changing inter-variable relationships; reducing all variables to one score can obscure this information. Future work should improve seasonal localization and pattern-type discrimination and study human-in-the-loop monitoring with variable-aware evidence. Causal root-cause identification requires additional domain knowledge and evidence beyond the observed univariate signal.

Reference construction.
Training sequences are partitioned into fixed-length segments. Segments overlapping binary-labeled anomalies are removed before prototype selection. The normal prototypes are observed subsequences selected with
k
k
-medoids; the number of medoids is chosen using a silhouette-based criterion, and small reference pools are retained without clustering. Each selected prototype produces one contrastive pair for each of the nine types. A pair stores the original and perturbed signals, the injected type, and a summary of statistical, change-point, spectral, autocorrelation, or segment-comparison evidence. DTW retrieval selects three normal prototypes, followed by type-based selection of their perturbations using Analyzer candidates. No test labels are used in these steps.

For example, 54% recall among 24 seasonal samples implies 13 detected cases after rounding, whereas 75% agreement cannot be obtained by rounding an integer number of correct types out of 13. Appendix D
Agent Prompt Specifications
The following prompts specify the experimental agent roles. They describe intended behavior rather than enforceable guarantees. In particular, the Detector prompt permits additional chart-supported candidates, whereas the Supervisor is instructed to preserve confirmed records.
Global Rules (shared)
1.
A data point is considered anomalous if it is part of a consecutive anomaly sequence, or if it shows a sharp drop or spike.
2.
A data point is considered anomalous if it remains below/above predefined normal thresholds for an extended period and falls outside the expected normal range.
3.
Typically less than 5% of points are anomalous. An empty anomaly list is expected and valid in most windows.
4.
Normal data can exhibit variability, which must not be mistaken for anomalies.
5.
Misclassifying normal data as anomalous can lead to critical failures. Exercise extreme caution. False positives are not tolerated.
6.
Only flag data as anomalous when supported by strong statistical or visual evidence.
7.
Anomaly interval outputs must be precisely located and must not be excessively long.
8.
Merge nearby anomalous points into segments. Output only segments with strong evidence.
9.
An empty anomaly list [] is valid and common. Do NOT fabricate anomalies to meet a quota.

When no analyzer candidate has strong supporting evidence, return an empty list.

G.3
Scoring anchors and evaluation interface
The rubric separates detection correctness (Accuracy) from whether a diagnosis fits the observed evidence (Validity), how clearly it is explained (Clarity), and its perceived practical value (Usefulness). Evaluators saw both true and predicted intervals, so these ratings assess the combined detection and explanation output rather than explanation text in isolation. Table
17
gives the score anchors, and Figure
6
shows the displayed evidence and rating controls.
Table 17:
Likert-scale criteria and anchor descriptions used in the human evaluation.
Criterion
Score
Anchor description
Accuracy
1
Major anomalies are missed, or many normal regions are falsely identified as anomalous.
2
Important anomalies are missed, or substantial false positives are present.
3
Major anomalies are detected, but some misses or false positives remain.
4
Most anomalies are correctly detected, with only minor misses or false positives.
5
All anomalies are correctly detected with no false positives.
Validity
1
The diagnosed anomaly type, severity, or explanation is entirely inconsistent with the observed data.
2
The diagnosis is largely inaccurate or weakly supported by the data.
3
Some parts of the diagnosis are plausible, but others are not well supported by the data.
4
Most diagnostic conclusions are plausible and grounded in the observed evidence.
5
All diagnostic conclusions are well supported by the data and closely align with expert judgment.
Clarity
1

2

3
The overall logic is understandable, but some parts are weakly justified or difficult to interpret.
4

The explanation is highly clear, coherent, and easy to understand, with strong evidence support throughout.

Diagnostic Case Studies
Figure
7
pairs each 400-point signal with SAGE’s confirmed intervals and analyst-facing report. Shading marks the reference anomaly interval; red crosses mark detected anomalous points. The report records the interval, candidate types, evidence-strength confidence, alarm level, and suggested follow-up checks. These examples illustrate the form of the diagnosis rather than estimate detection accuracy.
In the upper case, the oscillatory signal loses amplitude after approximately index 304 and ends with extreme spikes. SAGE reports overlapping structural changes over 304–388 and 315–388, then a separate spike interval at 389–399 with a global point-outlier label. In the lower case, regular oscillation over 0–199 collapses into a low-amplitude noisy regime; SAGE assigns one extended interval, 200–399, with structural and seasonal labels. The suggested checks, such as inspecting sensor integrity or events near a change point, are hypotheses for an analyst to investigate, not verified root causes.
