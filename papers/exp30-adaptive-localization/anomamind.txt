Abstract.
Time series anomaly detection is critical in many real-world applications, where effective solutions must localize anomalous regions and support reliable decision-making under complex settings. However, most existing methods frame anomaly detection as a purely discriminative prediction task with fixed feature inputs, rather than an evidence-driven diagnostic process. As a result, they often struggle when anomalies exhibit strong context dependence or diverse patterns. We argue that these limitations stem from the lack of adaptive feature preparation, reasoning-aware detection, and iterative refinement during inference. To address these challenges, we propose AnomaMind, an agentic time series anomaly detection framework that reformulates anomaly detection as a sequential decision-making process. AnomaMind operates through a structured workflow that progressively localizes anomalous intervals in a coarse-to-fine manner, augments detection through multi-turn tool interactions for adaptive feature preparation, and refines anomaly decisions via self-reflection. The workflow is supported by a set of reusable tool engines, enabling context-aware diagnostic analysis. A key design of AnomaMind is an explicitly designed hybrid inference mechanism for tool-augmented anomaly detection. In this mechanism, general-purpose models are responsible for autonomous tool interaction and self-reflective refinement, while core anomaly detection decisions are learned through reinforcement learning under verifiable workflow-level feedback, enabling task-specific optimization within a flexible reasoning framework. Extensive experiments across diverse settings demonstrate that AnomaMind consistently improves anomaly detection performance. The code is available at
https://github.com/Xiaoyu-Tao/AnomaMind-TS
.

Anomaly Detection, Agentic Workflow, Tool-augmented Reasoning
1.
Introduction
Time series anomaly detection (TSAD) plays a critical role in many real-world systems, where detected anomalies often trigger downstream actions such as alarms, diagnosis, or intervention
(
Zamanzadeh Darban et al., 2024
)
. In such settings, effective anomaly detection requires more than assigning anomaly scores to individual observations. Practical solutions need to localize anomalous intervals, interpret diagnostic evidence, and support decision-making under evolving contextual conditions with limited or uncertain information
(
Zhou et al., 2023
)
. As time series data become increasingly diverse and context-dependent, these requirements place growing demands on anomaly detection methods beyond pure predictive accuracy, calling for evidence-driven and decision-oriented detection paradigms
(
Schmidl et al., 2022
)
.
Despite substantial progress
(
Zamanzadeh Darban et al., 2024
;
Chandola et al., 2009
)
, most existing time series anomaly detection methods remain model-centric. They typically frame anomaly detection as a purely discriminative prediction task
(
Gupta et al., 2013
)
with fixed and predefined feature inputs, rather than as an evidence-driven diagnostic process. Such formulations rely on rigid feature representations and scoring mechanisms, offering limited flexibility during inference. As a result, these methods often struggle when anomalies exhibit strong context dependence, evolve over time, or require careful analysis across different temporal resolutions
(
Dong et al., 2024
)
. More fundamentally, they lack mechanisms to adaptively prepare informative features, reason over intermediate evidence, or revise earlier decisions as new information becomes available, which limits their effectiveness in complex real-world settings under concept shift.
We argue that these limitations stem from a fundamental mismatch between how anomaly detection is commonly formulated and how it is carried out in practice
(
Cheng et al., 2026
)
. In real-world scenarios, anomaly detection rarely reduces to a single scoring or prediction step. Instead, it typically unfolds as a sequential decision-making process, in which analysts progressively narrow down suspicious intervals, selectively examine informative signals, and refine judgments based on intermediate findings
(
Goswami et al., 2025
)
. Decisions made at earlier stages directly influence what evidence is gathered next and how subsequent analyses are conducted
(
Jiang et al., 2024
)
. However, such iterative and adaptive processes are difficult to capture within conventional model-centric formulations, which treat anomaly detection as a static mapping from inputs to anomaly scores and provide limited support for dynamic reasoning or decision revision during inference
(
Russell-Gilbert et al., 2024
;
Liu et al., 2025
)
. These observations motivate moving beyond isolated predictive models toward agentic time series systems that organize anomaly detection as a system-level reasoning and decision process, integrating evidence acquisition, analysis, and refinement within a coherent and adaptive workflow.
While this perspective motivates a shift toward agentic time series systems, realizing such an anomaly detection process in practice poses substantial challenges. Effective anomaly reasoning requires access to diverse and reusable analytical capabilities, which calls for the careful design of tool engines that support adaptive feature preparation and diagnostic analysis without relying on hard-coded, task-specific pipelines
(
Wu et al., 2026
)
. In addition, the detection workflow must be explicitly structured to support progressive decision-making, in which coarse localization and fine-grained analysis are coherently integrated rather than treated as isolated stages
(
Xu et al., 2022
)
. Such a coarse-to-fine workflow is essential for balancing efficiency, accuracy, and interpretability. Beyond workflow design, inference in agentic settings introduces fundamental challenges related to stability and optimization. While general-purpose models offer flexibility for open-ended reasoning and autonomous tool coordination, they often lack the inductive biases and task-specific learning signals required for reliable anomaly detection decisions
(
Alnegheimish et al., 2024
)
. Ignoring these complementary strengths and limitations can lead to brittle or inefficient inference behaviors. Addressing this challenge calls for a hybrid inference paradigm that explicitly separates flexible reasoning from task-specific decision learning, combining general-purpose models with explicit and verifiable learning objectives to enable effective anomaly reasoning.
To address these challenges, we propose AnomaMind, an agentic time series anomaly detection framework that explicitly formulates anomaly detection as a sequential decision-making process. AnomaMind operates through a structured agentic workflow that incrementally constructs diagnostic evidence and refines anomaly decisions over multiple steps. Rather than relying on fixed feature inputs, AnomaMind progressively localizes anomalous intervals in a coarse-to-fine manner and gradually augments detection via multi-turn interactions with reusable analysis tools for adaptive feature preparation and diagnostic support. Crucially, AnomaMind adopts a hybrid inference mechanism: general-purpose models handle tool invocation and self-reflective refinement, while core anomaly detection decisions are optimized through reinforcement learning under verifiable workflow-level feedback. This design enables AnomaMind to integrate flexible reasoning with task-specific optimization, while preserving effectiveness in complex anomaly detection scenarios.
Our main contributions are summarized as follows:
•
We reformulate the task of TSAD as a sequential decision-making problem, moving beyond discriminative formulations toward an evidence-driven diagnostic process.
•
We propose
AnomaMind
, an agentic time series anomaly detection framework that realizes this reformulation through a structured coarse-to-fine workflow, tool-augmented adaptive feature preparation, and a hybrid inference mechanism that combines general-purpose reasoning with task-specific anomaly decision learning.
•
We conduct extensive experiments across diverse time series anomaly detection settings, demonstrating that AnomaMind consistently improves detection performance and validating the effectiveness of agentic, tool-augmented reasoning for TSAD.
2.
Related Work
In this section, we first review conventional anomaly detection methods, and then introduce recent advances in LLM–based TSAD.
2.1.
Conventional Anomaly Detection
Existing time series anomaly detection methods span several modeling paradigms
(
Gupta et al., 2013
;
Chandola et al., 2009
)
. Early statistical approaches detect anomalies by assuming that normal time series follow predefined distributional or structural patterns
(
Hyndman and Athanasopoulos, 2018
;
Roberts, 2000
)
. Classical detectors model regular temporal components such as trend and seasonality, and identify anomalies by thresholding residuals between observed values and model-based predictions, as exemplified by ARIMA-based residual analysis
(
Rousseeuw and Leroy, 2003
)
. Density-based approaches estimate data distributions or relative sample densities and treat sparse observations as anomalies
(
Schölkopf et al., 2001
;
Breunig et al., 2000
)
.
These methods offer clear interpretability but rely heavily on handcrafted assumptions.
To alleviate this limitation, data-driven deep learning methods adopt representation learning to model temporal patterns more flexibly
(
Su et al., 2019
)
. Reconstruction-based approaches, exemplified by USAD
(
Audibert et al., 2020
)
, learn compact representations of normal time series using autoencoder architectures and treat large reconstruction errors as indicators of anomalies. In contrast, forecasting-based approaches, such as LSTM-based predictors
(
Chauhan and Vig, 2015
)
, model temporal dynamics by predicting future values from historical observations and identify anomalies through large deviations between predicted and actual outcomes.
2.2.
LLM-based Anomaly Detection
More recently, LLM-based approaches have been explored for time series anomaly detection (TSAD)
(
Bhat et al., 2025
)
. Early studies predominantly convert numerical sequences into textual representations and directly feed them into pre-trained LLMs for zero-shot or few-shot anomaly identification. Representative methods such as SigLLM
(
Alnegheimish et al., 2024
)
and prompt-based GPT-4 detectors
(
Liu et al., 2026
)
demonstrate that LLMs can capture coarse abnormal patterns without task-specific training, leveraging the rich prior knowledge. However, these detection-only approaches essentially operate as static input–output mappings, lacking explicit modeling of temporal structure and iterative diagnostic reasoning.
In contrast, training-based strategies introduce task-specific adaptation mechanisms, typically via parameter-efficient fine-tuning or learnable adapters, to better align with anomaly detection objectives
(
Dong et al., 2024
;
Jin et al., 2024
)
. A representative example is AnomalyLLM
(
Liu et al., 2024
)
, which targets TSAD by distilling anomaly-aware representations from a pre-trained LLM into a lightweight student network and detecting anomalies through teacher–student feature discrepancies. More recently, agentic and tool-augmented LLM frameworks have been proposed to further advance TSAD by reframing detection as a multi-step decision-making process. For instance, ARGOS
(
Gu et al., 2025
)
employs multiple LLM-based agents to iteratively generate, validate, and refine anomaly detection rules through interaction with external tools and contextual data sources.

3.2.
Thinking Process for Anomaly Detection
To better understand the limitations of static anomaly detection formulations, it is instructive to examine how anomaly detection is typically performed in practice. Rather than making an immediate and definitive judgment, anomaly detection often follows an iterative reasoning process that gradually constructs evidence and refines decisions
(
Pimentel et al., 2014
;
Chandrayan et al.,
)
. The process commonly begins with coarse screening, where the goal is to quickly identify regions (or intervals) that are potentially suspicious while filtering out large portions of evidently normal behavior
(
Jiang et al., 2025
)
. At this stage, decisions are intentionally conservative and uncertainty-aware, favoring broad candidate regions over precise localization. Subsequently, targeted analysis is performed on the selected regions. This stage involves selectively examining informative signals, such as statistical irregularities, temporal patterns, or contextual inconsistencies, to assess whether the observed behavior can be reasonably explained. Importantly, such analysis is adaptive: different regions may require different types of examination depending on their characteristics and surrounding context. Throughout this process, anomaly judgments are rarely final at the moment they are made. Early decisions are often provisional and may be revised as new evidence becomes available
(
Gu et al., 2024
;
Zhang et al., 2025
)
. Additional analysis can either strengthen an initial suspicion or invalidate it, leading to decision refinement or rollback. This iterative reasoning process highlights that anomaly detection is fundamentally evidence-driven and decision-oriented, rather than a single-pass scoring operation. Viewed from this perspective, time series anomaly detection can be naturally formulated as a sequential procedure of candidate localization, evidence construction, and decision refinement, motivating agentic approaches that explicitly model anomaly detection as a sequential decision-making process in real-world practice.
4.
The Proposed AnomaMind
Building on the sequential decision-making formulation and evidence-driven reasoning introduced earlier, we present AnomaMind, an agentic framework for time series anomaly detection. AnomaMind operationalizes anomaly detection through adaptive feature preparation, tool-augmented reasoning, and iterative decision refinement, as detailed in the following sections.
4.1.
Framework Overview
Figure
1
illustrates the overall framework of AnomaMind. The framework is built upon a unified toolkit that provides structured analytical capabilities for perception, evidence construction, and reasoning support, including interval localization, feature extraction, and knowledge memory. Leveraging this toolkit, AnomaMind organizes anomaly detection as a progressive decision workflow consisting of coarse-grained interval localization, fine-grained detection, and iterative refinement. Within this workflow, different functional roles coordinate to support sequential decision-making. To support this progressive workflow, AnomaMind adopts a hybrid inference mechanism that combines general-purpose LLM-based reasoning with task-specific model learning. The general-purpose LLM is responsible for autonomous tool invocation and self-reflection, while anomaly detection decisions are optimized through reinforcement learning under workflow-level feedback. The following subsections detail each component.
4.2.
Detection Tool Construction
To support evidence-driven and sequential anomaly detection, AnomaMind is equipped with a structured collection of analytical tools that provide complementary capabilities for perception, numerical analysis, and contextual reasoning. Rather than embedding all functionality into a single model, these tools are designed as modular and reusable components that can be flexibly invoked during the detection workflow. Each tool focuses on a distinct aspect of anomaly analysis, enabling coarse evidence acquisition, fine-grained verification, and semantic interpretation to be carried out in a coordinated manner. Together, they form the foundation of the tool-augmented workflow, allowing anomaly detection decisions to be informed by diverse and progressively refined evidence.

4.3.
Coarse-to-Fine Detection Workflow
AnomaMind realizes anomaly detection through a structured coarse-to-fine workflow that unfolds over multiple interaction steps.
Rather than producing anomaly decisions in a single pass, the workflow progressively constructs diagnostic evidence and refines decisions as new information becomes available.
The workflow is organized into four key stages, each addressing a distinct role in the detection process: coarse evidence acquisition to localize suspicious intervals, adaptive evidence construction to gather informative cues, reasoning-based anomaly detection to produce fine-grained decisions, and iterative refinement to evaluate and revise intermediate outcomes.
These stages are tightly coupled and executed in a sequential manner, allowing earlier decisions to guide subsequent analysis while enabling principled revision when necessary.
Together, this coarse-to-fine design provides a unified mechanism for efficient localization, context-aware analysis, and accurate decision-making under complex and evolving conditions.
Coarse Evidence Acquisition
The workflow begins with coarse evidence acquisition, whose goal is to efficiently narrow down potentially anomalous regions from the full time series.
At this stage, AnomaMind leverages visual perception to identify suspicious temporal intervals based on salient pattern irregularities, abrupt changes, or distributional shifts.
Rather than performing precise anomaly judgments, this stage prioritizes recall and uncertainty awareness, producing a set of coarse candidate intervals that are likely to contain abnormal behavior.
By filtering out evidently normal regions early on, coarse evidence acquisition significantly reduces the subsequent search space and enables focused analysis.
Adaptive Evidence Construction
Given the candidate intervals identified in the coarse stage, AnomaMind proceeds to adaptive evidence construction.
In this stage, large language models autonomously orchestrate tool interactions to gather informative numerical and contextual evidence relevant to the detected candidates.
The selection and invocation of tools are driven by intermediate observations and accumulated context, allowing different intervals to be analyzed using different diagnostic strategies.
This adaptive process enables flexible feature preparation and evidence accumulation, ensuring that subsequent detection decisions are grounded in task-relevant and context-aware information rather than fixed feature pipelines.
Anomaly Detection
Based on the constructed evidence, AnomaMind performs reasoning-based anomaly detection to produce fine-grained decisions.
This stage is responsible for assessing whether candidate intervals exhibit anomalous behavior, given the numerical features, structural patterns, and contextual cues collected earlier.
Unlike rule-based or purely discriminative approaches, the detection policy is optimized through reinforcement learning under workflow-level feedback, allowing it to adapt to the sequential and interdependent nature of detection decisions.
As a result, anomaly detection decisions reflect both local evidence and the broader diagnostic context accumulated across interaction steps.
Iterative Refinement
AnomaMind further incorporates an iterative refinement mechanism to evaluate and revise detection outcomes when necessary.
Through self-reflection, intermediate decisions are examined for potential inconsistency, uncertainty, or insufficient evidence.
If refinement is triggered, the workflow may return to earlier stages to acquire additional evidence, adjust analysis strategies, or reconsider candidate intervals.
This iterative process enables principled decision revision and prevents premature or brittle conclusions, allowing anomaly detection to progressively converge toward more reliable results.
4.4.
Hybrid Decision Mechanism
AnomaMind adopts a hybrid decision mechanism to support effective and stable inference across the coarse-to-fine detection workflow.
The key idea is to explicitly separate general-purpose reasoning from task-specific anomaly decision learning, allowing different components of the workflow to leverage their respective strengths.
Specifically, AnomaMind combines (i) general-purpose large language models for flexible reasoning and tool invocation, and (ii) a detection-specific anomaly decision module optimized through reinforcement learning under workflow-level feedback.
General–Specialized Hybrid Reasoning
In AnomaMind, general-purpose LLMs are responsible for driving reasoning-intensive and structurally flexible components of the workflow. These components include coarse-grained interval localization based on visual perception, autonomous invocation of feature extraction and diagnostic tools, and self-reflective assessment of intermediate detection results. Such stages require adaptive reasoning, dynamic control flow, and the ability to integrate heterogeneous evidence, which are well supported by general-purpose models without task-specific retraining. By contrast, these components do not require precise anomaly decision boundaries or strong task-specific inductive biases. Instead of enforcing rigid prediction objectives at this level, AnomaMind leverages the expressive reasoning capability of LLMs to flexibly explore suspicious regions, construct informative evidence, and guide subsequent decision steps. This design avoids over-constraining early-stage reasoning and enables the workflow to adapt to diverse anomaly patterns and contextual variations.
Reinforcement Learning for Anomaly Decision Learning
While general-purpose reasoning is effective for evidence construction and workflow control, accurate anomaly detection decisions demand task-specific learning signals and stable optimization for precise boundary localization.
To this end, AnomaMind introduces a dedicated anomaly detection module whose decision policies are trained through reinforcement learning. Unlike prior approaches
(
Cheng et al., 2025
)
that apply multi-turn reinforcement learning to train the entire detection workflow end-to-end, AnomaMind restricts reinforcement learning to this local decision module
(
Li et al., 2025
)
, significantly reducing training complexity. The reinforcement learning objective is defined based on workflow-level feedback rather than isolated prediction errors. Specifically, rewards are computed by evaluating the quality of anomaly detection decisions in the context of the full workflow, taking into account factors such as consistency with verification evidence, alignment with diagnostic outcomes, and the effectiveness of subsequent refinement steps. By grounding rewards in workflow-level feedback, the learned detection policy captures task-relevant inductive biases while remaining compatible with the surrounding reasoning-driven workflow.

Implemention Details
Our framework consists of several specialized components. For fine-grained reasoning, we employ Qwen3-8B
(
Bai et al., 2023
)
as the backbone, while all other modules use grok-4 as the default LLM. The workflow is optimized via full fine-tuning within the agent-lightning framework, with a learning rate of
2
×

2\times 10^{-5}
and a batch size of 8, and trained on four NVIDIA A800 GPUs (80GB). We follow official implementations and hyperparameters for all baselines.
The goal of TSAD is to detect true anomalies while minimizing false alarms, requiring both high recall and precision. We therefore adopt F1 as the primary metric and additionally report Best-F1 by sweeping the detection threshold over a predefined range, following standard TSAD practice.
5.2.
Main Results
As shown in Table
2
, AnomaMind consistently demonstrates competitive average performance across the four evaluation metrics. The performance gains are particularly evident on datasets with more complex anomaly characteristics, where static detection methods often struggle to adapt to evolving temporal patterns and contextual dependencies. A closer examination across different baseline categories reveals several notable advantages of the proposed framework.
Conventional single-pass architectures exhibit limited effectiveness in these settings, suggesting that static input–output discrimination is insufficient for capturing evolving temporal contexts. Foundation models, while effective at achieving high recall, tend to suffer from reduced precision in zero-shot settings, reflecting a tendency to flag benign irregularities as anomalies in the absence of task-specific adaptation. In contrast, AnomaMind attains a more balanced precision–recall trade-off by adopting an agentic reinforcement learning strategy that enables the internalization of task-specific inductive biases.
Furthermore, compared with existing LLM-based detectors that lack explicit verification mechanisms and often display unstable numerical sensitivity, AnomaMind benefits from a tool-augmented reasoning workflow that supports iterative refinement and decision revision. Overall, these results suggest that reframing anomaly detection as a sequential, evidence-driven decision process—supported by adaptive reasoning, feedback-driven refinement, and specialized tool interaction—provides a promising alternative to conventional model-centric paradigms. conventional model-centric paradigms conventional model-centric paradigms

Workflow Component Ablation.
As shown in Figure
2
, removing the evaluator results in a consistent performance degradation across all datasets, highlighting its importance in the proposed coarse-to-fine detection workflow. The degradation is more pronounced on IOPS and WSD, suggesting that the evaluator plays a key role in validating intermediate detection hypotheses and reducing false alarms through structured verification. In the absence of this component, detection decisions rely solely on coarse signals and are more susceptible to error accumulation across steps. By providing explicit feedback to refine earlier decisions, the evaluator enables the full framework to perform more reliable iterative refinement, leading to more stable detection performance across diverse time series scenarios.

5.3.3.
Reinforcement Learning Ablation.
As shown in Figure
3
, removing reinforcement learning results in a consistent and substantial performance degradation across all evaluated datasets, highlighting its importance in the proposed framework. Without reinforcement learning, the model relies primarily on fixed supervision signals and lacks the ability to revise or correct earlier decisions based on downstream outcomes.
By incorporating reinforcement learning, the agent is able to exploit outcome-driven feedback to refine its decision-making strategy over multiple steps. This allows the model to adjust its reasoning trajectory, suppress false alarms, and progressively improve anomaly judgments beyond coarse initial predictions. As a result, the full framework achieves consistently higher F1 and Best-F1 scores across datasets. Overall, these results indicate that reinforcement learning plays a crucial role in enabling adaptive policy optimization and iterative decision refinement within the agentic anomaly detection workflow.

5.5.
Case Studies
Figure
4
illustrates a representative example of the proposed tool-augmented, multi-turn agentic anomaly detection process on a periodic time series. The framework first performs coarse localization by combining visual cues with global sinusoidal trends, identifying a suspicious interval where a sharp acceleration disrupts the expected smooth pattern.
The locator then applies domain-aware verification by focusing on rate-of-change statistics while discarding unstable amplitude-based signals. Guided by this strategy, the actor extracts quantitative evidence using global and local difference Z-scores, revealing a dense outlier cluster indicative of sustained acceleration. The detector integrates multi-level evidence to confirm a violation of the smoothness constraint, and the evaluator further refines the anomaly boundaries by checking logical consistency across visual observations, tool outputs, and domain constraints. The final output is a refined anomaly interval with an interpretable diagnosis, demonstrating the effectiveness of multi-turn reasoning and tool interaction for precise anomaly detection.
6.
Conclusion
In this work, we revisit TSAD from an agentic perspective and argue that effective anomaly detection should be treated as an evidence-driven diagnostic process rather than a static discriminative task. We propose AnomaMind, an agentic TSAD framework that reformulates anomaly detection as a sequential decision-making process with tool-augmented reasoning. By organizing detection into a structured coarse-to-fine workflow, AnomaMind enables adaptive feature preparation through multi-turn tool interactions and improves detection via self-reflective refinement. A key contribution is the hybrid inference mechanism, which decouples flexible reasoning from task-specific decision optimization, allowing general-purpose models to orchestrate tools while anomaly detection policies are learned through reinforcement learning under verifiable workflow-level feedback. Extensive experiments across diverse benchmarks demonstrate consistent performance gains, especially in complex scenarios. We believe AnomaMind represents a promising step toward more reliable, interpretable, and extensible TSAD systems and highlights the broader potential of agentic, tool-augmented reasoning.

Appendix A
Dataset Details
To rigorously evaluate the effectiveness of our method, we selected four widely used benchmarks in the field of time series anomaly detection. These datasets span from real-world production data to diverse synthetic scenarios, covering a broad spectrum of anomaly types, including point, contextual, collective, and pattern anomalies. A detailed description of each dataset follows:
•
YAHOO
: The Yahoo S5 dataset (2015) serves as a standard benchmark for univariate time series anomaly detection, derived from Yahoo’s production traffic. It comprises a real-world subset (A1) featuring genuine traffic spikes and drops, alongside three synthetic subsets (A2, A3, A4). These synthetic series are designed to mimic realistic temporal patterns while providing precise control over point, contextual, and collective anomalies.
•
TODS
: The TODS benchmark is a synthetic dataset designed to evaluate outlier detection under diverse and controlled scenarios. It contains univariate series featuring five distinct anomaly types from a behavior-driven taxonomy: point-global (single extreme deviations), pattern-contextual (locally anomalous subsequences), pattern-shapelet (unusual motifs), pattern-seasonal (disrupted periodicity), and pattern-trend (long-term trend deviations).
•
IOPS
: The IOPS dataset (AIOps KPI Anomaly Detection dataset) is a widely adopted real-world benchmark for evaluating anomaly detection in cloud and web service monitoring. It features Key Performance Indicators (KPIs) such as traffic, latency, and CPU usage, reflecting the health and scale of massive online systems. The dataset contains hundreds of labeled anomalies—including spikes, drops, and drifts—encompassing point, contextual, and collective types, making it essential for assessing automated incident detection in complex IT environments.
•
WSD
: The WSD dataset is a real-world benchmark consisting of KPIs collected from large-scale internet companies, including metrics like traffic, response times, and error rates. It reflects actual system health in high-traffic environments and is characterized by realistic production noise and a very low anomaly density (
∼
\sim
0.6%). The data includes labeled spikes, contextual shifts, and long-term drifts grounded in real incidents, often set against pronounced periodic and seasonal patterns.
Appendix B
Baseline Details
To comprehensively evaluate our method, we benchmark against 10 competitive approaches, categorized into statistical methods, deep learning models, foundation models, and LLM-based agents.
Statistical Approaches.
FFT
detects anomalies by decomposing time series into frequency components. It retains low-frequency coefficients to reconstruct a smoothed signal, identifying anomalies through large deviations in high-frequency residuals.
SR
(Spectral Residual) utilizes the Fourier transform to calculate log-amplitude deviations from local averages in the frequency domain. It then reconstructs the saliency map via inverse transform to pinpoint anomalous points.
Deep Learning Baselines.
CNN
employs 1D convolutional layers to extract local temporal features and forecasts future values via fully connected layers, detecting anomalies based on prediction error thresholds.
LSTMAD
utilizes an LSTM-based encoder-decoder architecture to learn latent representations of normal patterns. It identifies anomalies by thresholding reconstruction errors, as deviations yield significantly higher losses.
TransAD
leverages a Transformer encoder-decoder with multi-head self-attention to capture global temporal dependencies, using reconstruction error for anomaly detection.
M2N2
is a test-time adaptation method that handles distribution shifts. It detrends sequences via exponential moving averages and selectively updates parameters on low-error instances through self-supervision.
Foundation Models.
TimesFM
is a decoder-only foundation model that patches input sequences into fixed-length tokens. It autoregressively generates future values, detecting anomalies via point-wise prediction errors.
OFA
(One Fits All) utilizes a frozen pretrained transformer backbone with lightweight task-specific adapters. It processes sequences through the frozen encoder and uses adapter heads to reconstruct inputs for anomaly identification.
Chronos
is a language-model-based forecaster that tokenizes time series values. Operating in a zero-shot setup, it produces probabilistic forecasts via a transformer encoder-decoder, flagging anomalies based on prediction deviations.
LLM-based Approaches.
LLMAD
employs in-context learning by retrieving similar time series segments as exemplars. It feeds these into an LLM with chain-of-thought prompts to directly infer anomaly locations, types, and explanations.
LLM-TSAD
decomposes time series into trend, seasonal, and residual components, converting them into text descriptions. Using few-shot prompts, the LLM reasons over the decomposed signals to identify anomalies.

D.2.
Sample Construction
For traditional self-supervised and semi-supervised baselines, we adhere to the TSB-AD benchmark protocol, utilizing the anomaly-free initial segment of the test sequence for training. In contrast, for our proposed method, we incorporate additional distinct sequences from the same dataset as training samples to enhance generalization.
To balance the model’s capacity for processing long-term context with computational efficiency, we set the sample length to 100 and segment the time series using a step size of 100.
